# How to turn subject expertise into an article cluster

You’ve narrowed the kitchen cabinets down to two oak finishes. The pale one looks lovely by the window. So does the smoked one, rather inconveniently. You take them both home. A week later you’re back at the counter, quite sure which one you want, and the designer hands you a form. Hang on. Which of these little numbers says you’re getting *that* oak?

If you run the showroom, you’ve just found two questions worth writing about. Someone choosing a finish could use a side-by-side comparison. Someone with a pen in their hand needs to know where to check the sample number before they sign. Put those guides together in the blog category for your fitted-furniture service and you’ve started an article cluster. You’ve also saved the poor soul with the pen another tour of the colour options.

Shall we try it with your business? Open your service page and the articles you already have. Then invite the person who actually does the work to bring along something they can show you. A blank form is plenty. There’s no need to borrow a customer’s paperwork for the occasion.

## Put the samples on the table

For the rest of this example, let’s borrow that imaginary showroom. It already has a finishes guide. Sit down with the designer, put the pale oak beside the smoked oak and ask, ‘If I took these home tonight, where would you tell me to look at them?’ Follow up with, ‘What might still surprise me when the cabinets arrive?’ Those answers give your writer something much more useful than two photographs of brown things.

Now pick up the form. Ask the designer to show you where the sample number goes and what happens if it doesn’t match the one you chose. A minute ago you needed both pieces of oak. Now you need the little number on the back of just one. That’s a good reason to give sign-off a guide of its own.

Jot down what the designer showed you and who can check the explanation before it goes public. The samples can illustrate the difference between finishes. The person responsible for the current form can tell you what the signature covers. Keep private paperwork out of your illustrations. Google’s [guide to helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) offers a useful editorial question here: what will someone learn from your first-hand knowledge that they couldn’t get from a quick summary elsewhere?

## Find a home for each question

In our client work, we’ve built separate service pages for distinct types of work. Those pages are a handy place to start. If a customer simply wants to know whether you fit cabinets in a particular kind of room, put the answer where they can enquire. Making them hunt through the blog for it is a bit much.

Here’s what I’d give the writer in our fictional showroom. Try reading down the first column as if you were the customer. By the fourth row you may feel you’ve been here before. Quite right, too.

| Customer question | Evidence to inspect | Where the answer belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can I order fitted cabinets for this room? | Which rooms the team fits and what they need to give a quote | Answer this where people ask for a quote. |
| How do I compare two finish samples at home? | Two samples you can show and the designer’s notes | Put the two samples side by side in the finishes guide. Ask the designer what a customer should look for at home. |
| What exactly am I approving when I sign off a sample? | A blank copy of the form customers receive | If your blog doesn’t cover this yet, write a short guide. Show where to find the sample number and who to ring if it’s wrong. |
| Which cabinet finish should I pick? | Same samples, same decision as the comparison guide | Use the finishes guide again. It’s the same customer choosing between the same two pieces of oak. |

If you’re still deciding how to organise the category itself, our [content hub guide](/blog/build-a-content-hub-people-can-use/) will help. Once it’s in place, you can add the questions customers actually ask. Two ways of asking which finish to choose can share a perfectly good answer.

## Give the writer something they can put to the test

“Write about sample approval” sounds perfectly reasonable until you’re the one staring at the blank page. I’d hand the writer a blank copy of the form and a note like this.

> Our reader has chosen a cabinet finish and received the approval form. Use the current blank form to show them where to find the sample reference, what they’re being asked to confirm and who to call if the numbers don’t match. Ask the person who sends out the form to check your instructions. Link back to the finishes guide for anyone still weighing up the two samples.

You can test that brief before you publish. Give a colleague the draft, the form and a sample with a different reference. Ask them what they’d do next. If they spot the mismatch but have to ask you whom to call, you know exactly which sentence the draft is missing. You can borrow the template in our [guide to writing a content brief](/blog/content-briefs-search-intent-internal-links/).

Once someone’s picked a finish, where would they want to go next? Pop a link there that says “check what you’re approving”. Give the person who’s still torn between pale and smoked oak a way back to “compare the finishes”. Google’s [link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) explains why wording that names the destination helps people find their way.

## What if you already have two guides to oak?

Lay the two guides side by side and cover their titles. Read the opening, look at the example and try the last instruction. Could you swap the pages around without the reader noticing? Keep the clearer guide and bring the useful bits across. On the other hand, if one reader is off home with the samples and the other is fishing about for a pen, each guide has earned its place.

If you use Search Console, pick a date range in the Search results performance report, filter to a relevant query and open **Pages**. Read the pages that appeared. A service page and a finishes guide can both make sense for a broad cabinet search. If someone looking for sample sign-off lands on a page full of lovely oak photographs and no mention of the form, you’ve found something worth fixing. Seeing two URLs for one query won’t, on its own, tell you that either is losing traffic. Google’s [Performance report documentation](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553) explains the filters and which queries may be missing.

## Try one row with your own expert

[Download the article cluster worksheet (CSV)](/downloads/expertise-article-cluster.csv). The first three rows follow our showroom example. Duplicate the blank row and fill it in for one real customer question. Who at your business knows the answer? Write their name beside the question, then paste in the link you’d send a customer today. If the page nearly answers it, jot down what’s missing. If you can’t find a page you’d happily send, you’ve got your next writing job.

Once the page is live, hand your phone to someone who missed the meeting. Start them in the category and ask them to find the answer, use the example and show you what they’d do next. Write down where they hesitate. That’s a much more useful edit for tomorrow morning than dreaming up a third title about oak.
